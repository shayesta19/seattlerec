Seattle is a strip of hills wedged between two bodies of water. Get that in your head and the rest of the city snaps into place.

**West is salt water.** Elliott Bay, opening into Puget Sound, opening into the Pacific. This is the side with the ferries, the working waterfront, the sunsets. If you can see the Olympic Mountains, you are looking west.

**East is fresh water.** Lake Washington, twenty miles long, with Bellevue and the rest of the Eastside on the far shore. Two floating bridges cross it — I-90 to the south, SR-520 to the north. They float because the lake is too deep and too soft-bottomed to hold conventional piers.

**In the middle is Lake Union**, small and busy, ringed by houseboats and seaplanes and kayak rentals. It connects to both the other two through a canal and a set of locks, which is why you will occasionally watch a sailboat sail through the middle of a neighborhood.

**South is Mount Rainier**, and on a clear day it does not look real. It is 14,410 feet, roughly 60 miles away, and locals call it "the mountain." When somebody says *the mountain is out*, they mean the clouds have cleared and you should go look right now.

**I-5 runs north–south** through the whole thing and is the spine of every direction anybody will ever give you. North of downtown, south of downtown — that is how the city is described.

## The one thing that will trip you up

It is **hilly**. Not San Francisco hilly, but enough that a route that looks like a ten-minute walk on a map can be a genuine climb. Queen Anne, Capitol Hill, Beacon Hill, First Hill, Magnolia — the names are not decorative. Check the elevation before you commit to walking somewhere, and know that downtown's east–west streets go steeply uphill from the water.
